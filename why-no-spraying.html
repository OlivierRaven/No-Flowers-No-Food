Why not spraying

Routine herbicide and pesticide use is sold as “tidy”, “necessary”, and “safe”. It isn’t. Here’s why spraying harms Aotearoa’s insects, soils, waterways, and people — and what to do instead.

The case against chemicals

Spraying does more harm than good

Silent Spring (Rachel Carson, 1962) warned that chemical shortcuts create long-term damage. Decades later, we still see the same patterns: residues in soils and water, collapsing insect life, and a costly “chemical treadmill”.

Ecological harm

Sprays drift and run off. They kill non-target insects, amphibians, and soil microbes, and fragment food webs.

Human health

Chronic, low-dose exposure is under-tested. Residues can persist on food, playgrounds, and verges.

Pollinator loss

Bees, hoverflies, butterflies, and moths are sensitive to many common actives and “inert” co-formulants.

Resistance & treadmill

Pests evolve. Doses increase. New formulations arrive. Costs rise while ecosystems weaken.

Fake “tidiness”

Browned-off edges may look “managed”, but they remove flowers, trap litter, and signal neglect.

Intergenerational costs

Contamination lingers for years. Restoring soils and waterways is slow and expensive.

Answering the sales pitch

Common industry claims — and clear replies

“Modern sprays are targeted and break down quickly.”

Field reality is messier: drift, runoff, and repeated applications create chemical “cocktails”. Many impacts occur at sub-lethal doses (navigation, reproduction, immunity). “Targeted” biochemistry is shared across species.

“Safe when used as directed.”

Most testing is short-term and industry-provided. Chronic, low-dose, and mixture effects are under-represented — precisely what communities experience.

“We need pesticides to feed the world.”

Food insecurity is driven by waste, distribution, and fragile monocultures. Agroecological systems maintain yields while protecting biodiversity — and cut long-term costs.

“Weed-free verges are safer and tidier.”

Flower-rich verges can be safe and more resilient. Use mosaic mowing, keep edges/pathways neat, and add small signs. You get habitat, pollinators, and lower erosion and fire risk.

“New formulations solve resistance.”

They postpone it. True resilience comes from diversity: varied plant communities, groundcovers, and habitat for natural enemies.

Better practice

What to do instead (Aotearoa-first)

Native-first planting

  • Hebe (koromiko), mānuka/kānuka, harakeke, olearia, kōwhai
  • Layer tussocks, shrubs, and trees for year-round nectar and shelter.

Mosaic mowing

  • Mow 2–3×/year, never all at once; keep tidy edges and sightlines.
  • Avoid mowing Oct–Jan (peak insect season).

Weed without chemicals

  • Hand-weed high-visibility areas; mulch or plant densely to shade out “weeds”.
  • Use groundcovers on edges to reduce maintenance.

Habitat, not bare soil

  • Leave leaf litter, logs, and brush piles.
  • Create stone stacks and nest pockets for insects.

Community + signage

  • Explain the approach (“Let Nature Grow” signs).
  • Monitor with photos; share results with council and neighbours.

Policy pilots

  • Ask councils for reduced-spray zones and flower windows.
  • Offer to help monitor biodiversity outcomes.
A reminder

From Silent Spring

“The ‘control of nature’ is a phrase conceived in arrogance.” — Rachel Carson

After the Second World War, a new chemical called DDT was praised as a wonder spray. It killed mosquitoes, flies, and farm pests almost instantly. People used it on crops, gardens, streets, and even inside homes. At first it seemed harmless — there were fewer insects and cleaner-looking fields — but no one looked closely at what else it was doing.

In the years that followed, scientists began to notice strange things. Fish were dying in rivers after nearby spraying. Earthworms disappeared from the soil. Most worrying of all, birds like robins, owls, and eagles were laying eggs with shells so thin they cracked before the chicks could hatch. In some areas, young birds that did survive were weak and couldn’t even stand on their legs because the chemicals had made their bones soft.

In 1962, marine biologist Rachel Carson gathered the evidence and wrote Silent Spring. She showed that DDT and other pesticides didn’t stop at killing pests — they built up in every living thing and moved through the food chain, from insects to birds to humans. The “silent” spring she described was real: whole places where the birds had stopped singing.

Her book shocked the world and led to public investigations, new environmental laws, and finally the ban of DDT in many countries, including New Zealand. But even after more than sixty years, the same pattern continues — new sprays, new promises, and the same hidden harm.

We can choose to learn from that history. By planting natives, mowing less, and skipping chemical sprays, we can keep our towns and farms alive with bees, birds, and real spring song again.
